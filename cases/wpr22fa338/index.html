PiperSport (N126WK)

Stall and spin after a go-around at Santa Monica Airport September 8, 2022, 16:26 PDT · Santa Monica Municipal Airport (SMO)
Accident
Serious outcome
2 fatal / 2 aboard
No survivors
Investigation status
Final report published
NTSB · Released July 10, 2024

Case summary

An introductory flight lesson at Santa Monica Flyers ended when a hard landing led the instructor to attempt a go-around. The airplane pitched up almost vertically, climbed, then stalled and spun to the left before striking the ground. Both the flight instructor and the passenger — who was taking his first flight, a gift from family, and held no pilot certificate — were killed. The investigation found the passenger was inadvertently pressing his microphone button while gripping the controls, which may have kept the instructor from being heard as he tried to recover.

What happened

Source: NTSB Final Report (excerpt)

Multiple witnesses observed the airplane land on runway 21, with one describing the landing as "hard." The witnesses stated that the airplane then began to aggressively pitch up and climb while the engine made a sound consistent with it going to full power. All the witnesses provided similar accounts of the airplane continuing to climb in a nose-up attitude before leveling off at the apex of the climb, about 100-200 ft agl, then spinning to the left, descending, and colliding with the ground. A security video camera located on the southeast side of the airport captured the final landing approach segment. As the airplane came into view, the main landing gear was already on the ground and the airplane then began to climb in a slightly nose-up attitude. Over the next three seconds, the airplane pitched up to an almost vertical attitude and climbed out of the camera's field of view. The shadow of the airplane on the runway surface indicated that it continued to climb for another two seconds before descending and striking the ground about 5 seconds later. Audio recording of the airport's CTAF captured the flight instructor responding that it was to be a full-stop landing, and a few seconds later the audio captured him screaming, "let go, let go……. let go, let go, let go."

Why it happened

Source: NTSB Final Report
NTSB probable causeNTSB

"The pilot's inability to maintain control of the airplane during the landing flare due to the passenger's excessive control inputs, which resulted in the airplane exceeding its critical angle of attack and entering an aerodynamic stall."

Contributing factors
  • Hard landing leading to an abrupt go-around
  • Passenger's excessive aft control input during the go-around
  • Airplane exceeding its critical angle of attack and entering an aerodynamic stall
  • Passenger inadvertently holding the push-to-talk switch, which blocked the instructor's radio calls and may have delayed recognition of the emergency
  • Instructor's inability to regain control before ground impact
Safety takeaways
On introductory/discovery flights, agree explicitly on who holds the controls during the critical landing and go-around phases — don't assume a passenger will let go on command once control forces build up.
Anxious first-time flyers can grip the controls far harder than expected — the FAA's own guidance warns learners can be "incredibly strong" and react inappropriately under stress; brief this risk before every introductory flight, not just experienced-student flights.
A positive exchange of flight controls ("I have the flight controls," confirmed verbally) should be practiced and enforced on every flight with a non-pilot aboard, especially during a go-around.
Be aware that intercom/PTT design can let a panicking occupant's grip on the stick silence the pilot's own transmissions at the exact moment communication matters most — know your aircraft's intercom behavior.
A go-around after a hard landing is already a high-workload maneuver; with an unbriefed or startled passenger on the controls, be prepared to take over immediately and firmly rather than attempt to talk them off the controls first.
